01 / THE PROJECT

More than a kit.

Engineering is a cycle of ideas, prototypes, testing, and decisions. The process leads to a better understanding of the problem and a more effective solution.

2026ROBOT SEASON
IntakeFEATURED SUBSYSTEM

Project description: The 2026 FRC robot rebuild focused on creating a reliable, competition-ready machine while adapting the organizer-provided KitBot intake to a MAXSwerve drive base. With a small, inexperienced team and limited budget, every design decision had to balance performance, available materials, and what we could actually build.

My goal: I aimed to make the intake reliable: it needed to acquire balls, retain them, feed them consistently, and function without restricting the shooter or compromising robot clearance.

My role: I contributed to mechanical design, fabrication, programming, team organization, and leadership. I helped track limited resources, develop the intake concept, gather feedback, build prototypes, conduct tests, document results, and guide the team through design changes.

My process: I followed an iterative eight-step process that began with the 2026 game rules and available resources. I helped select an idea, gather feedback, plan revisions, build a cardboard prototype, test and improve it, integrate it with the robot, and complete final practice cycles.

My solution: I helped adapt the standard KitBot intake to the MAXSwerve base using revised height, pitch, width, mounting, wider intake wheels, foam retention, and a hopper net. An intake delay also synchronized ball feeding with shooter spin-up.

02 / HOW THE WORK HAPPENS

From first sketch
to field-ready thinking.

FRC robot building is a cycle of strategy, design, fabrication, programming, testing, and refinement. Each stage informs the next until the robot can perform reliably under competition conditions.

01
DEFINE RESOURCES

Start with what is available.

We reviewed the game rules, parts, tools, materials, time, and team skills before choosing a direction. I helped track our budget and resources so we knew what was possible with what we had and what we still needed.

↗
02
INITIAL IDEA

Choose a direction.

Because we were a new team with limited funding and tools, we prioritized a simple, affordable design. We used the manufacturer-provided KitBot intake as a starting point and planned how to adapt its narrower geometry to the wider, taller MAXSwerve base using materials we already had. The intake began as the manufacturer planned design KitBot intake and was retrofitted from the KitBot drive base to the MAXSwerve drive base. Its height, pitch, mounting hardware, and width were adjusted so the originally lower, narrower concept could work on the (now) wider, taller square base. But noting our lack of tools for fabrication and limited aluminum stock, we planned to prototype and manufacture the spacers from used wood and polycarbonate as those were plentiful and much easier to work with.

↗
03
GATHER FEEDBACK

Improve the idea together.

Our design team shared the concept with teammates, drivers, and mentors. Their feedback revealed limited electrical space, repairability concerns, likely failure points, and the need to work with the mounting hardware we already had.

↗
04
PLAN

Turn feedback into action.

Plan the mounting changes, revised height and pitch, wider base fit, materials, and build order. Implement the planned changes while keeping the design serviceable and compatible with the rest of the robot. Our shooter intake waas not built to be this high off the ground and so it would not be able to reach the balls, nor have the intended scooping action with the bottom metal scoop. So we planned to lower the intake to be able to reach the balls and scoop them up. Knowing the geometry was built for straight on flat ground, we also planned to angle the intake forward to avoid pushing the balls away when going over the raised section of the field. We also planned to add wider intake wheels to grab the balls with friction.

↗
05
OFF-ROBOT PROTOTYPE

Build the essentials first.

We built a cardboard prototype to check the geometry, roller spacing, game-piece control, and hopper-to-shooter handoff. This let us find problems and make changes before spending time and materials on a full build.

↗
06
TEST + ITERATE

Learn from every run.

Test the bare prototype, gather feedback, and adjust the design until it satisfies the initial goal. After mounting it we realized that this was not a good idea as it would be too low and the angle would cause the intake to hit the trench when going over the raised section and break. To counter this, we raised the intake to a more reasonable height giving it enough clearance to avoid the trench and angled it forward to make it easier to avoid pushing the balls away, and added wider intake wheels to grab the balls with friction. This allowed the intake to be much taller than orignally designed for but ideally would still be able to pick up balls from the ground. I helped lead the testing and iteration process by gathering data, considering the resources available, and directing the team to make changes to the design to improve its performance. One instance of this was when we realized that the intake was leaking balls and we needed to add foam to prevent this. I suggested adding leftover foam to the intake while wrapping it in canvas to prevent the balls from rolling out. We then shaved the foam down until it was able to retain the balls while not blocking the shooter. And this foam was exteremly easy to work with, cost effective, and the nature of its compressibility and the anti rip fabric allowed it to last throughout the season without needing to be replaced.

↗
07
ROBOT INTEGRATION

Test the real constraints.

Install the prototype that satisfied the initial goal on the robot, gather feedback on its implementation, and test it with the drive base, hopper, shooter, controls, and other subsystems. Continue gathering feedback and iterating on the design until the full system meets the goal. Our shooter was inconsistent with its trajectory and spin up when under load from multiple balls and the intake was not able to feed the balls into the shooter consistently. To solve this I programmed a delay in the intake to allow the shooter to spin up before feeding the balls into it. This prevented jamming as balls could no longer get stuck in the intake between it and the shooter as they would not be fed in a state when the balls would trap the flywheel and prevent it from spinning up.

↗
08
FINAL BUILD + PRACTICE

Manufacture, install, and prepare.

Once the planned changes are complete and the goal is satisfied, manufacture the final design with its intended materials. Install the final mechanism, perform a final test, and practice repeated robot cycles so the team is as close to ready to use in competition. Once the intake and shooter were working together, I helped manufacture and install the final design. I then practiced repeated cycles to prepare the team for competition. The process remained a loop: one problem could lead back to resources or planning for another design change. For example, quick stops could fling balls out of the hopper. I helped add a flexible net across the top to contain them, allowing the robot to stop quickly without losing game pieces. The side bonus of this is that it also prevents the balls from rolling out of the hopper when driving over the raised section of the field and increased capacity as the balls would be contained within the hopper instead of bouncing out.

↗
03 / FEATURED SUBSYSTEM

The intake
iteration story.

A working mechanism takes more than a good idea. The intake connects physical design with software behavior, making it a useful case study in cross-disciplinary engineering.

My role: I helped lead the intake's transition from concept to a competition-ready mechanism by coordinating feedback, prototyping, testing, design changes, and programming.

My solution: The intake began as the standard KitBot intake and was retrofitted to the MAXSwerve drive base for the competitive advantage of swerve drive. That retrofit required adjusting the intake's height, pitch, and mounting hardware, while adapting its narrow geometry to the wider square base.

To improve reliability, foam was added to keep balls from rolling out of the intake. The foam was experimentally shaved until it consistently prevented rollout while still allowing balls to feed from the hopper into the shooter.

The finished intake had to balance several competing requirements: it needed enough contact to control the ball, enough clearance to avoid restricting the feed, and enough structural support to stay aligned while the robot moved. Testing focused on repeatable acquisition, controlled retention, and a clean handoff into the hopper and shooter.

After the off-robot prototype met its initial goal, it was mounted to the MAXSwerve robot and tested alongside the drive base and other subsystems. Driver feedback from those tests guided the final adjustments before the design was manufactured with its intended materials and used in practice cycles.

05 / WHAT COMES NEXT

Every iteration
is information.

My impact: The impact of my work is visible throughout the project in both leadership and design decisions. I led testing, organized feedback, recommended foam retention, directed geometry revisions, and programmed the shooter synchronization delay. Those decisions helped turn a standard KitBot mechanism into a practical, more reliable intake for our MAXSwerve robot.

The work also strengthened the team's ability to communicate changes, use available materials, practice repeated cycles, and continue improving after every test. The foam retention, hopper net, and programming delay addressed real competition problems, including ball rollout, jam prevention, and quick-stop retention.
